# *Quercus robur*

**English oak · Pedunculate oak**  
**Stieleiche** · **Chêne pédonculé**



## Overview

*Quercus robur* is one of the characteristic broad-leaved trees of European forests and landscapes. It is a large, long-lived deciduous tree with a broad crown and a massive trunk when mature.

It is native to much of Europe and occurs in forests, woodland edges, hedgerows, parks and river valleys. Old oak trees are particularly valuable for biodiversity, providing food and habitat for insects, fungi, birds and mammals.

## Characteristics

### 🌳 Tree and crown

A mature *Quercus robur* develops a broad, rounded or irregular crown with large branches. Old trees can become extremely large and develop dead branches and cavities that provide valuable wildlife habitat.

### 🪵 Bark

Young trees have relatively smooth, greyish bark. With age, the bark becomes darker, thicker and deeply fissured, forming ridges and grooves that support lichens, mosses, fungi and small animals.

### 🍃 Leaves

The leaves are simple and deeply lobed. They have a **very short leaf stalk** and characteristic rounded lobes at the base.

The combination of lobed leaves and a very short stalk is useful for identifying the species.

### 🌼 Flowers

Male and female flowers occur on the same tree. The male flowers form hanging catkins, while the small female flowers occur near the leaf axils.

Flowering takes place in spring, around the time the new leaves emerge.

### 🌰 Acorns
![Quercus robur — leaves and acorns](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/af/Quercus_robur.jpg)

The fruit is the familiar acorn. A particularly useful identification feature is that the acorns are carried on **long stalks (peduncles)** — hence the name "pedunculate oak".

Acorns mature in autumn and provide an important food source for birds and mammals.

### 🌱 Buds and shoots

The buds are relatively small and occur around the ends of the twigs. Their appearance, together with the branching pattern and bark, can help identify the tree during winter when there are no leaves.

## Habitat and distribution

*Quercus robur* is native to much of Europe and extends into western Asia.

It can be found in:

- deciduous and mixed forests
- woodland edges
- hedgerows
- parks and old wood pastures
- river valleys and floodplains

It generally grows well on fertile soils and is particularly characteristic of lowland landscapes.

## Seasonal cycle

**Spring:** New leaves emerge and the tree flowers.

**Summer:** The crown is fully developed and the acorns develop.

**Autumn:** Acorns mature and fall. The leaves are shed.

**Winter:** The tree is leafless, making the bark, buds and branching structure useful for identification.

## Ecological importance

Old oak trees support an exceptionally rich community of organisms. Their leaves and buds provide food for insects, while bark, dead wood, cavities and decaying branches provide habitats for fungi, lichens, invertebrates, birds and mammals.

Because oaks can live for centuries, a single old tree can develop many different habitats throughout its lifetime.

## Identification summary

Look for the combination of:

- Large deciduous tree
- Broad, spreading crown
- Deeply fissured bark when mature
- Deeply lobed leaves
- **Very short leaf stalk**
- Rounded basal leaf lobes
- Acorns on **long stalks**

The **short leaf stalk + long acorn stalk** combination is particularly useful when distinguishing *Quercus robur* from other European oaks.
